Transcript of Introductory Comments Delivered at CyberSpaceII conference in July of 1995

Over a year ago a group met in the Virtual Corporation section of CompuServe's Working From Home forum. We began an exploration of what a virtual corporation is and what advantages it might offer info-workers. A great deal of examination centered on the nature of electronic communication and how to effectively engage others, daily and for the long term, with nothing but ASCII text to represent each other.

As such, many of our discussions centered on how to refine our writing skills to accommodate the potentials and pitfalls of electronic communication. Early on it was recognized that diversity of view points would be needed to ensure that all angles of a given problem would be explored. But diversity of views means, necessarily, contention of views. How could we conduct ourselves such that, in our efforts to present our own views as cogently and persuasively as possible we didn't neglect a viewpoint that might be needed to compensate for our own blind spots? How could we elicit insights of other points of view?

Some of the conclusions (tentative and inviting of examination) arrived upon could hold value to educators as they engage peers and students in the electronic realm. How might instructors nurture an appreciation for diversity? How could collaborative learning projects stimulate the imagination of students and their commitment to embracing valuable insights no matter where they are found?

This presentation examines the role e-mail can play in group (as opposed to one-on-one) interaction. E-mail can be narrowly defined as the asynchronous exchange of messages. When messages are broadcast to a well defined group of people who share a common purpose or frame of reference, a Mailing List is born. When those individuals begin to ask questions of other members threads develop, positions agree and conflict, and it becomes a complex of group interactions.

Need for alternative writing style

Because the dynamics of computer-mediated groups differ so significantly from conventional or RW (real world) interactions it can be useful to recognize the differences between RW writing and writing for an electronically meditated group. Computer-Mediated Communication (CMC) has too many variables to safely assume that the compositional styles instilled in prep and underclass composition classes prepare people to be effective communicators in the electronic realm.

By way of demonstration, consider your fatigue right now if you are reading this paper off your computer screen. The writing style has been not unlike what one expects of college papers; longish sentences, composed from a perspective of the "known" on the part of the writer seeking to "educate" the reader, an "academic" tone.

Whatever validity this type of writing holds for college students addressing instructors via term papers (where the goal is to impart information) the style doesn't translate well into the electronic realm. The reason is not simply a matter of physical medium either.

(Now I'll shift gears on you and write in a style the might be called online-ese.)

A composition is static, something agonized over, rewritten, revised until it is ``finished''. For good or ill, electronic writing is more of a /meta/position than /com/position...more of a conversation inviting feedback than a solitary jewel of prose inviolate in its form and function.

Since a mailing list is a form of group interaction, a writing style that takes group processes into account is needed. The rest of this paper looks at some of the differences imposed by the medium (wire rather than paper). The presentation will offer some techniques that can be useful in writing more effectively for the electronic realm.

Advantages of electronic interaction

Time Shifting

That e-mail is a convenience should be readily apparent. The fact that a posting may sit for weeks, months and even years waiting to be read liberates both sender and receiver. It's accessible to the whole group at the leisure of its individuals. The practical effect, however, is paradoxical. People tend to post and respond at a furious, almost real-time pace rendering messages of a few days ago potentially irrelevant today.

Even so, the ability to consider and compose replies clearly separates e-mail from the demands of the face-to-face. A VCR lets you bend the schedule of the broadcast network to your own -- an e-mail account lets you seemingly hold a conversation with folks half-way across the world when you're both awake.

Ego Shifting

Writers, freed from immediate feedback (read: rejection) have fewer inhibitions than other social settings. This encourages people to "try on" different points of view than they might otherwise put forward. It takes less gumption to play devil's advocate.

Another effect of this dissociation from physical feedback and cues is the encouragement of introspection and intimacy. [Since you can't interrupt me, I'll take as long as I need to make my point. Since I don't have cues that let me know you're understanding me, I'm forced to be as clear and concise as possible. Since I'm committing my thoughts to text, I have to take examine more consciously what message I want to send.]

All these factors work together to encourage the writer to self-examination at the same time it emboldens self- expression. In this sense it is a shared journal...an open diary.

Focus on Text

``It's all ASCII to me.'' Perhaps because we are so far removed from the days of IBM Selectrics and mimeograph machines and have been bombarded by the works of mediocre desktop publishers and fancy fonts, 3the funneling of thoughts down to the barest essentials brings a focus to the medium. The effect is similar to looking at a black and white photograph and realizing one can see more clearly the important elements without the distractions of color. It's no accident that shades of gray carry the weight of the message.

Disadvantages

For each section above there is an inverse. The pace of interactions can leave threads worthy of further exploration unexamined. Barring one's soul to a group of strangers carries risk. The lack of graphics and the inability to denote emphasis with bold or italic typeface makes full communication harder. Therefore it's incumbent on participants to be tolerant and empathetic in order to realize true communication.

Flames are inevitable. Jerks will occasionally gain admittance to the group and cause havoc. It's all part of the process. But maintaining a conscious and questioning perspective at all times can allow masters of the medium to turn even these downsides to their advantage.

Conclusions

The deliberative nature afforded by the medium allows users to construct contexts rather than forcing them to accept (or be subjected to) empirical assumptions. Groups that interact long enough to develop a certain amount of history will begin to use a shorthand to communicate nuances and shades of gray. To turn a phrase of C.J. Jung's; think of this as the `Collective Assumption.'

If one shifts focus from "I possess knowledge that I want to give to you" to "Here's my perspective and why I hold it, what's your reaction?" the result is an invitation to an extended conversation. The dynamics of this interaction moves the group forward.

The goal should be on gaining insights not to demonstrate how clever, right, righteous one is or how ignorant, unreasonable, misinformed the other is.

Given the assumption that the individual has a responsibility to the group to compose posts the move the process forward, the conference presentation will offer some techniques to that end.
