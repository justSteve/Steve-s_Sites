Below is the foundational essay I provided for Virtual Online University in December of 1995 while Director of Communications.   Unfortunately cirumstances relating to the veracity of the Executive Director caused myself and most of the rest of the factulty to dissolve our relationship with VOU in the summer of 1996. This essay is included here since it so completely represents my vision for an Online Educational Environment. - Stephen Hueners; March 1998

Virtual Online University, Inc.
AN OVERVIEW

Virtual Online University is a non-profit corporation which engages in two educational missions: Athena University for Higher Education and Athena Preparatory Academy for pre-university education as well as functioning as a consultant to corporate trainers and educational institution seeking an effective distance educational delivery system. VOU represents a new model for education and training by providing real-time, computer-mediated classes from an electronic campus.

While this is novel in and of itself, this institution is not founded within an electronic medium simply because it can be. We feel we've identified some inherent deficiencies with education as it has evolved in the late 20th century and hope to make a contribution that benefits the entire educational community; students, faculty, and administration.

The Status Quo: What's Wrong?

The `chalk and talk' approach to education - the traditional classroom experience bound by physical walls - brings with it many elements that work to impede effective learning and teaching.

  • Students and teachers are both limited by geographical distances and time constraints
  • Students are trained to be passive learners and note-takers
  • The artificial distinction between The Classroom and The Real World perpetuates the false dichotomy between living and learning
  • Instructional materials, in particular text books, are two dimensional at best and frequently out-dated upon delivery.

All-in-all, traditional educational venues encourage an authoritarian, monolithic experience which discourages most students from becoming self-directed learners and active participants. The culture thus created has obvious implications as these students become employees and government officials: people who have been taught to fill-in-the-blank with the right answer; to not question the answers that they've been authorized to provide; who've not been taught how to construct meaningful links between seeming desecrate, isolated facts.

The pace of change today (social, economic and technological) demands professionals who understand how to identify interconnected fields of knowledge where those connections exist and (more importantly) how to create connections where they don't. This skill demands an understanding of collaboration, which in turn demands critical thinking.

Our Vision: The Way We Think It Should Be!

We base our vision on these precepts:

  • Students learn best when they are active participants in their own educational process;
  • Students should be served by the educational institution (and not the other way around), especially students who are place- or time-bound;
  • The student-teacher relationship is paramount in the learning process;
  • Teachers must function as mentors to students rather than as the sole source of authority;
  • Education must provide a context for living and decision-making, and should offer the student life-skills beyond the content of the curriculum;
  • The educational process must be interactive between teacher and student and between student and student;
  • Education is best facilitated when engaged in the context of a learning community;
  • Significant learning occurs when students can make connections across disciplines and are able to integrate those disciplines into a cohesive whole;
  • The educational environment should be global in reaching out to faculty and students from diverse cultures and geographical regions while simultaneously encouraging students and faculty to maintain their own cultural individualism.

VOU seeks to integrate the collaborative process with the educational process by bringing students and teachers together from all over the world into a virtual environment, accessed by the Internet and mediated by computers. Instructional materials are delivered in hypertext, encouraging students to make connections beyond the specific material at hand and requiring critical examination of each subject of study.

We chose the Internet as our instructional delivery medium not because it is new or popular, but because advanced telecommunications technologies offer a rich venue to both instructors and students to concentrate on what should be at the center of their interactions: the interconnectedness of the facts and ideas under examination. Distractions are eliminated and the pursuit of knowledge is distilled down to its essence.

Our students and instructors are encouraged to function as generalists seeking to connect arts and sciences into an integrative world view - the humanist ideal which briefly captivated European culture in the Renaissance. We seek to extend that original ideal using the electronic resources of the Internet by providing a place where learners interact -literally- with knowledge. The student constructs and internalizes a given body of knowledge that is directly and immediately relevant because it is presented in relation to other bodies of knowledge.

Essentially we are blending a vital, time honored educational model with technological enhancements available today in a way where each is consistent with the other. Our educational philosophy is mirrored by our educational technology in a harmonious, complementary fashion.

By inviting the student to actively participate in goal setting and measurement we hope to motivate a self-directed, self-interested search on the part of the individual. By utilizing the Internet resources of the entire globe we expect that this self-directed quest will create additional connections with both like-minded and divergent seekers. Note how this mirrors the very process that has created the whole range of Internet infrastructures and embellishments.

Such is the philosophical basis for our model. Now let's examine the technical specifics used to implement that philosophy.

Overview of the Technology

The electronic campus is a community comprising students and teachers from anywhere there is an Internet connection: it is open and available 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. Students may work through materials on the World Wide Web or work with other students on campus at anytime. Rather than lecturing, teachers conduct online seminars and facilitate discussions in a Socratic dialogue; all sessions are recorded and logged, making available to students who cannot attend the sum and substance of the real-time activities.

Asynchronous interaction (email) broadens the scope of the classroom and facilitates student-student and student-teacher interaction beyond the boundaries of the seminar. Finally, students build a set of valuable research and computer skills which are applicable to a world in which computers and the ability to access and integrate information are becoming increasingly important.

Our Electronic Campus

In constructing our campus we chose a mix of technologies that are cross platform (Mac PC/Windows and Unix); that don't require expensive, high speed network access; and are based on widely used, mature technologies. But we also needed a robust media capable of transmitting text, graphics and audio as well as something to allow direct, real-time group interaction.

Interactive groups is the key. We believe the over-riding trend in Internet technology is one of increasing interactively. The first widely available "interface" for Internet access was Gopher (navigation via menus) which evolved -conceptually if not directly- into the World Wide Web (navigation via hypertext links). WWW is far more powerful than Gopher but it's still reactive - not truly interactive.

Our instructional space is constructed on an Internet program called a Multi-User, Object Oriented environment: MOO.

MOOs - The Foundation Technology

MOOs are text-based, virtual reality sites that allow people to connect to the same place at the same time. They are completely unlike conventional chat rooms you might be familiar with in that they allow the manipulation and interaction with cyber-objects in addition to just chatting with other people.

MOOs are widely used public domain programs meaning they are stable, mature environments. The biggest challenge facing a MOO user is lack of graphical information. VOU has partnered with a software developer to incorporate a state of the art HTML front end to our MOO.

To fully appreciate the implications of using a graphical front end it's necessary to understand how users interact with the conventional MOO. Recall that we've said people interact on a MOO by transmitting text. Let's consider what a classroom exchange might look like: 

Bertsch nods to Bill. 

Bill says, "well, consider the effect that the long bow had
on warfare...."a simple little tech..." 

Bill says, "it altered not only the way that battles were
waged, it altered the way that communities were formed...."

Bertsch says, "Good example." 

Bill says, "the old "siege" concept didn't work
anymore, so communities relied less on extravagant walled cities."

margit says, "absolutely" 

margit says, "however, Bill, you are looking back at that..and
here I am asking us to look ahead...the technologies are whirring
around us and we need to get a hold of them I think" 

Joyce waggles her hand at the teacher 

margit asks, "Joyce?" 

Bill says, "Well, I would say that the same thing is happening
to us" 

Bertsch says, "I believe we could have predicted how the
long bow would have changed warfare." 

Joyce says, "why do I need to consider all this stuff..."

margit says, "excellent question" 

Bertsch [to Joyce]: Because we cannot, I feel, predict how the
advent of computers into education will change things. 

[and so on]

So long as the subject of study can be represented by plain text, the group can share information quite easily. And because they are "talking" using text they are generating study notes at the same time, but more importantly they are interacting more directly with the ideas than might otherwise be the case. This is a critical, central tenant of our education philosophy (and yet another example of how our educational technology reinforces and facilitates that philosophy) - by interacting via textual expressions learners process information at a higher cognitive level than speaking/hearing.

Multi-media Capabilities Increase the Depth of Interaction

Understanding the power of text-based interactions (and remaining committed to its primacy) we can safely (and productively) introduce the use of graphics and audio. A MOO capable of transmitting graphics and audio means our group above can extend their conversation to include graphics, audio, even hypertext links....anything that can be transmitted with HTML code is now available to extend their interaction.

In a very literal sense, when learners incorporate HTML elements into their interactions then extend the very language they use. The implications are as wide ranging as they are unknowable. But one thing is certain: This model enhances the educational process. While we do not claim that this model completely replaces conventional educational models, we are convinced it offers some very viable alternatives for a great many "non-traditional" students and an exciting supplemental modality for all.

Shared Web/MOO Space

Developments currently underway will automate the generation of much of the HTML code that users see. For example, WebObjects is a cross platform application ideal for constructing HTML code in real time. We hope to use this technology to link multiple MOO servers into a more integrated environment. This will lighten the processing load on the MOO allowing better performance and the creation of a network of MOOs.

Understanding the interactions between the MOO and WebObjects requires exploring a few simple principles:

  • A MOO is a dynamic, self regulating database depicting objects and their relationships. Most importantly it facilitates group interactively but is limited to simple textural representations.
  • Conventional HTML is media rich (audio, graphics, animation as well as formatted text) but is solitary and static.
  • WebObjects allows us to integrate the two models giving us access to the strengths of each while mitigating their weaknesses.

When fully implemented WebObjects will give our MOO full automation of routine maintenance tasks, be scaleable and distributable, and will be able to send users a whole range of specialized programs (via Java) regardless of the computer connecting the user to the Internet.

Implications for Education

In the above scenarios, a few concepts become clear. First of all, the isolation of the distance education student is removed as students and teachers become a part of a virtual community. They do not receive packets in the mail from an impersonal instructor nor do they sit and watch a television screen while a teacher lectures. Rather, they are active, participating members.

Secondly, students and teachers alike are able to integrate the environment with instructional materials such that learning and living become a seamless cloth. Instructional materials may be accessed from home, work or from anywhere that the student can use a computer. The Internet has been compared to a global library, and it is within this library that students learn and interact, pulling into the seminar materials which exist at their very fingertips.

Finally, the limitations place on geographical location are removed and students can receive instruction from highly qualified educators regardless of their physical location.

Above all, we recognize the importance of making and strengthening connections. Whether between individuals and groups, between people and the technology they use, or between people and ideas, we've created a space that facilitates those connections by literally building a web that weaves all those components together.

Students who participate in our dynamic environment emphasizing peer collaboration and active learning not only learn the subject matter more thoroughly, they are better prepared to play a productive role it today's team-oriented, inter-dependent and inter-connected environment.

Implications for Corporate Training Solutions

The information just presented to you in this document is clearly focused on an academic model and you may be wondering how this applies to the corporate trainer. Our intent has be to establish that our work is grounded in a rich, viable, educational model. As we said earlier, we don't expect this model to be the best learning solution for every type of student. But the corporate trainer is faced with precisely the sort of student who will excel under this model: the mature, self-direct, self-motivated learner who understands how important it is to learn and work within teams.

We feel we offer several other important features beyond educational effectiveness of critical importance to the corporate trainer:

  • Low cost, low bandwidth;
  • Modular solutions from a common infrastructure;
  • Working from within a technology (internetted interactivity) that will be able to easily embrace and integrate the enhancements (better audio, graphics, video, and connectivity)
